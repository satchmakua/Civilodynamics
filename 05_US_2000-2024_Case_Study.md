## Abstract

We apply Civilodynamics — a framework that models societies as complex dynamical systems and treats civilizational stress as the primary structural variable behind political outcomes — to the United States over 2000–2024. The framework represents a society's stress state as a vector across six blocks (economic, elite, legitimacy, cohesion, epistemic, demographic) reduced to a scalar Civilizational Stress Index (CSI). Using public-domain data (World Inequality Database, Bureau of Labor Statistics, Pew, Gallup, Federal Reserve, and associated sources), we present an *illustrative* CSI trajectory and argue that the six stress blocks were jointly elevated and rising **before** the political developments of 2015–2016 that candidate-centered accounts treat as causes. We advance, as the central hypothesis, that anti-systemic political candidacy achieving major-party success is a structurally probable output of high-stress states rather than a candidate-specific anomaly — and we state precisely how this hypothesis, and three more contested sub-hypotheses it generates, could be falsified. We develop a structural comparison with the Weimar Republic (1919–1933), framed explicitly as **hypothesis-generating analogy, not validation**, with its disanalogies given equal weight. We register five timestamped, Brier-scorable predictions for 2026–2030. All CSI values are illustrative pending the computation specified in Document 4; the substance under test is the qualitative structural account and the registered predictions. We do not dispute that candidate-specific and contingent factors influenced outcomes; we argue for structural *primacy* as a testable claim, and we treat the registered predictions as its arbiter.

**Keywords:** civilodynamics, structural-demographic theory, political instability, anti-systemic politics, elite overproduction, epistemic coherence, Civilizational Stress Index, poliform transition, U.S. political economy.

## 1. Introduction

The U.S. electoral outcomes of 2016 and 2024 generated an extensive literature of candidate-centered explanation: the liabilities of specific nominees, late-breaking information events, the Electoral College, polling error. These accounts are not wrong, and this paper does not claim they are. It asks a different and complementary question: were the conditions that produced these outcomes measurable in advance, and do they constitute a structurally probable *class* of civilizational behavior rather than a sequence of contingent accidents?

This paper argues for the **structural-primacy hypothesis**: that in high-stress civilizational states, the structural demand for anti-systemic political expression rises substantially, that this demand exists in the electorate prior to and independently of any specific candidate, and that the candidate who channels it is a secondary contingency relative to the structural condition. This is offered as a hypothesis with stated falsification conditions (§4), not as an established result, and it is held alongside — not against — candidate-centered explanation: structural conditions set the possibility space; candidate factors operate within it and shape margins.

The most proximate antecedent is the structural-demographic theory of Turchin and colleagues (Turchin 2003, 2016; Turchin and Nefedov 2009), which identifies elite overproduction, popular immiseration, and state fiscal stress as primary drivers of political instability. Civilodynamics extends this in the three narrow ways set out in the charter (Document 1): an explicit epistemic/information layer, the poliform–morphospace integration, and a real-time measurement ambition. The framework's debt to cliodynamics is foundational and is acknowledged as such.

In the poliform language of Document 3, the United States is an instance of the **industrial nation-state** basin, and the sharp structural question this paper poses (§6) is whether the elevated stress of 2008–2024 is an *intra-basin* secular-cycle peak that will relax, or the early signature of a *between-basin* transition toward the algorithmic-surveillance basin. The paper does not resolve this question; it frames it and registers predictions that bear on it.

The paper proceeds: §2 states the framework briefly; §3 presents the component-level stress trajectory and an illustrative CSI; §4 gives the retrospective interpretation as a set of falsifiable sub-hypotheses, with alternatives; §5 develops the Weimar comparison as hypothesis-generating analogy; §6 poses the poliform question; §7 registers five predictions; §8 states limitations; §9 concludes.

## 2. Framework (in brief)

The full specification is in Documents 1, 2, and 4. The minimum needed here:

**The stress readout and the CSI.** A society's stress state is a vector $\mathbf{u}(t)$ across six blocks — economic stress, elite dynamics, legitimacy, cohesion, epistemic coherence, demographic pressure — each built from operationalized variables drawn from public data (the full variable table and sources are in Document 4 §3). The CSI is a scalar reduction of $\mathbf{u}$ measuring distance from a defended stable baseline.

**The baseline and its contestability.** Variables are normalized against a U.S. stable-period baseline of 1948–1973 (post-war convergence: falling inequality, broadly rising real wages, comparatively high institutional trust). This baseline is contestable, and the paper acknowledges it directly: the same period was marked by racial apartheid, Cold War anxiety, and gendered institutional exclusion. The choice is a methodological commitment subject to sensitivity analysis (§8), not a free parameter, and the baseline-covariance problem it creates is resolved per Document 4 §4 (a coverage-restricted baseline vector plus a modern-era epistemic extension).

**The CSI's correct interpretation.** The CSI is reported here as a per-dimension-normalized composite (Document 2 §4.5), not as a raw Mahalanobis distance read against the 2σ/3σ rule. This matters: a raw multivariate Mahalanobis distance is chi-squared distributed and a baseline-typical state already sits well above zero, so the original threshold labels were miscalibrated. The corrected reading is qualitative-magnitude-preserving — the trajectory still rises — but the specific numbers and their thresholds await the computation of Document 4.

**Anti-Systemic Political Event (ASPE).** Defined in advance to avoid post-hoc classification, and deliberately broad: (a) a candidate without prior major-party institutional career achieving major-party presidential nomination; (b) a candidate campaigning on institutional delegitimation exceeding 35% of the two-party popular vote; or (c) a third-party/independent candidacy exceeding 15% of the popular vote. The core hypothesis treats elevated CSI as a **necessary but not sufficient** condition for ASPE occurrence — a claim that, as §4 notes, requires a stated functional form and base rate to be genuinely falsifiable, which the predictions of §7 supply.

## 3. The U.S. stress trajectory, 2000–2024

The component-level figures below are largely **sourced reported statistics** (and should be verified against current provider data at submission); their normalization and aggregation into a CSI is **illustrative/estimated** pending Document 4's pipeline.

**Economic stress.** Real wages for the bottom two quintiles were effectively flat from 1979 through approximately 2015 even as productivity rose (Mishel et al. 2015; EPI wage-productivity series). Top-1% net wealth share rose from roughly 23% (1980) toward roughly 38% (2020) per the Federal Reserve DFA, approaching late-1920s levels (Saez and Zucman 2020), corroborated by WID. Intergenerational mobility declined over the period (Chetty et al. 2014). *(Specific transition rates cited in the original — e.g., bottom-to-top-quintile probabilities — should be re-verified against the Opportunity Insights series before submission; they are not load-bearing for the trajectory.)*

**Elite dynamics.** The Elite Overproduction Index (construction and caveats in Document 4 §6) rose materially over the period. Law-school first-year enrollment rose to a peak near 52,500 around 2010 (ABA) while lawyer-position growth lagged; PhD conferrals rose toward roughly 55,000/year by 2010 (NCES/SED) while tenure-track availability in the humanities and social sciences contracted after ~2008, widening the credential-position gap. Turchin (2016) identifies elite overproduction as among the most predictive instability variables in his historical set. *(EOI values are estimated pending the harmonized protocol of Document 4 §6, which must bridge the 2000/2010 occupational reclassifications.)*

**Legitimacy.** Gallup's institutional-confidence series shows mean confidence across consistently-tracked institutions falling from roughly 48% (mid-1970s) toward roughly 27% (2023); Congressional confidence specifically fell to single digits (~8%, 2023). Pew's parallel series shows accelerating partisan divergence in trust after ~2008.

**Cohesion.** Population-weighted county partisan lean increased over the period (Bishop 2008; Chen and Rodden 2013), reducing routine cross-partisan contact; affective polarization rose and, per Iyengar et al. (2019), exceeds issue-based polarization and accelerated after ~2000.

**Epistemic coherence.** This is the weakest-measured block (Document 4 §5). The Media Overlap Score, estimable with quality only from ~2012 and reconstructed earlier, declined over the period; the Factual Consensus Rate **has no instrument yet** and its pre-instrument values are not reported as data. The composite ECI is estimated to have moved from a functional into a degraded range over ~2012–2014, but this estimate rests on interpolation and is explicitly indicative, not measured.

**Demographic.** Youth-cohort and rural-urban divergence measures (Census ACS; ERS) contributed secondary, slower-moving pressure.

That claim rests on the component evidence (wage stagnation, wealth concentration, rising elite overproduction, legitimacy collapse, early epistemic fragmentation), most of which is reasonably solid and pre-2015, and it is falsifiable: if computation shows the blocks were *not* jointly elevated before 2015, the structural-primacy hypothesis loses its principal support.

## 4. Retrospective interpretation: 2016 and 2024 as a set of testable hypotheses

This section states the structural interpretation as falsifiable sub-hypotheses rather than assertions, gives the alternatives equal hearing, and ties each hypothesis to the registered predictions that test it.

### H1 — Structural precedence (the core claim)
*Statement:* Stress was elevated and rising before the 2015–2016 developments, such that anti-systemic emergence is better explained as a structurally probable output of a high-stress state than as a candidate-specific anomaly. *Operationalization:* the six stress blocks jointly above baseline and trending upward by 2012–2015. *Way to be wrong:* computation (Document 4) showing the blocks were not jointly elevated/rising before 2015 falsifies H1.
*Supporting context, stated even-handedly:* the anti-establishment signal in 2016 was visible across multiple candidates of both parties before any one became dominant, and broadly contemporaneous anti-establishment outcomes appeared in several democracies (Norris and Inglehart 2019) — consistent with a common structural driver. *But the cross-national pattern is not uniform:* several democracies under comparable structural stress did **not** produce equivalent anti-systemic outcomes in the same window, which is itself evidence that structure sets a possibility space rather than determining outcomes. H1 claims primacy of structure, not sufficiency.

### H2 — Centrist structural disadvantage
*Statement:* In high-CSI environments, institutionalist (incremental-reform, pro-institution) candidacies face a structural headwind independent of the personal qualities of the specific candidate. *Operationalization:* the vote share of institutionalist candidacies declines as CSI rises, controlling for candidate-quality proxies, across cases. *Way to be wrong (stated in the original and retained):* an institutionalist candidate achieving a decisive popular-vote victory in a CSI-high environment falsifies H2. *This is directly tested by Prediction 1.* The institutionalist nominees of 2016 and 2024 (Clinton; Harris) are the U.S. instances; H2 makes a claim about the *environment*, not about those individuals' competence or policy positions, which it treats as secondary.

### H3 — Delegitimation-signal inversion
*Statement:* In collapsed-legitimacy environments, signals of institutional disapproval (indictments, elite condemnation, mainstream-media opposition) can function as positive credentials of anti-systemic authenticity for a segment of the electorate. *Operationalization:* among voters with low institutional trust, exposure to institutional-disapproval signals is associated with non-negative or positive candidate evaluation, versus negative association among high-trust voters. *Way to be wrong:* if, across measurement, institutional-disapproval signals depress support uniformly regardless of institutional-trust level, H3 fails. *Caveats given equal weight:* the counterfactual is unobservable (we cannot run the 2024 election without the indictments), the net effect may be mixed and masked by other factors, and the claim is among the most contested in the framework. The paper states it as a hypothesis precisely because it is not established. **This is a structural description of a hypothesized mechanism and carries no normative endorsement of any candidate, outcome, or conduct.**

### The information layer — mechanism, not intent
The framework holds that engagement-optimizing information environments amplify emotionally arousing, identity-confirming, low-accuracy content, degrading the shared factual commons and increasing the electoral fitness of candidates optimized for algorithmically-amplified engagement over institutional competence. This **mechanism** is operationalizable (via the ECI, Document 4 §5) and is retained. The paper deliberately does **not** advance the stronger claim that this degradation was *coordinated or deliberately engineered*: that is a causal-intent claim that is far harder to establish, pulls toward unfalsifiability, and is not needed for the structural argument. The mechanism is the claim; intent is not asserted.

### Alternatives the paper does not dispute
Candidate-specific factors (nominee liabilities, communication styles), contingent events (information shocks, the pandemic's timing), and institutional features (the Electoral College, primary dynamics) all influenced outcomes and margins. The structural account claims *primacy* — that these operated within a possibility space the structural conditions defined — not *exclusivity*. Distinguishing structural primacy from these alternatives is exactly what the registered predictions (§7) are designed to do over the coming cycles.

## 5. Historical comparison: the Weimar Republic, 1919–1933

> **Epistemic status — read this first.** The Weimar comparison is **hypothesis-generating analogy, not validation.** It is a sample of one, scored qualitatively. It cannot validate the CSI scale, because no CSI has been computed for Weimar on commensurable variables; doing so is deferred to the Retrodiction Casebook (Document 6). The original framing of this comparison as "a form of cross-civilizational validation" is withdrawn. The parallels suggest hypotheses; the disanalogies are given equal weight; nothing here implies the United States is on the Weimar trajectory.

**Structural parallels (qualitative).** Across economic stress (sustained immiseration and wealth divergence), elite overproduction (a frustrated credentialed cohort), institutional legitimacy (a delegitimated parliament/Congress), and epistemic fragmentation (a party-press commons in Weimar; an algorithmic one now), the two cases show directionally similar structural conditions. The *mechanisms* differ sharply — a partisan press versus algorithmic curation — even where the structural outcome (a fragmented factual commons) rhymes.

**Critical disanalogies (given equal weight).** The Weimar constitution had specific structural weaknesses (Article 48 emergency powers; no constructive vote of no confidence) that the U.S. system does not share in the same form; U.S. federalism, an independent judiciary, and civil-society density provide resistance Weimar lacked. The German military's institutional ambivalence toward democracy has no direct contemporary U.S. parallel. The Weimar monetary catastrophe (hyperinflation then deflation) has no U.S. counterpart. And Weimar's organized paramilitary mobilization (SA, SS, Reichsbanner) at scale is not present in the contemporary U.S. case, though January 6, 2021 is a qualitatively novel U.S. data point warranting structural monitoring. These disanalogies justify the conclusion that the U.S. system, while in a high-stress state, is **not** in Weimar's terminal trajectory — and they justify treating the comparison as a source of hypotheses, not a forecast.

## 6. The poliform question

Translating into the poliform framework (Document 3): the United States is in the **industrial nation-state** basin. The central structural question is whether the elevated stress of 2008–2024 is (a) an **intra-basin secular-cycle peak** — a Turchin "Age of Discord" that, however severe, relaxes within the basin — or (b) the early phase of a **between-basin transition** toward the algorithmic-surveillance basin, driven by a regime change in the information axis (a Type-2 basin-reshaping in the terms of Document 2 §6.3).

Two honest caveats sharpen this. First, the industrial nation-state is the *weakest convergence case* in the catalog (Document 3 §5): it arose from few origins and spread by diffusion, so claims about its "natural" instabilities are less secure than for, say, the steppe-nomad confederacy. Second, the algorithmic-surveillance basin is *untested* — possibly a genuine new attractor, possibly a transient, possibly an artifact of shared technology diffusing across different political traditions. The framework does not claim to know which. It claims only that the question is now answerable in principle, and the registered predictions bear on it: sustained ECI decline and continued anti-systemic consolidation would be consistent with (b); relaxation of the stress blocks would be consistent with (a).

## 7. Prospective predictions, 2026–2030

> Registered April 2026; carried forward **unchanged** in this revision, retaining the original timestamp. Resolution criteria are clarified for precision where needed but the claims and probabilities are not altered. The probabilities are **calibrated subjective estimates** informed by the structural assessment and by analogy to historical cases — they are not outputs of a fitted model (no model is yet fitted), and they therefore carry model uncertainty. All are Brier-scored at resolution; updated estimates, if any, will be published as separately-timestamped versions.

**Prediction 1 — P = 0.72.** At least one major-party presidential or vice-presidential nomination in the 2028 cycle goes to a candidate whose platform includes explicit institutional delegitimation of at least one co-equal branch of government. *Resolution (Nov 2028):* documented campaign statements of a nominated candidate explicitly calling for subordination, defunding, restructuring, or delegitimation of the judiciary, legislature, or executive *as an institution* (not mere policy disagreement with current officeholders). *Tests H1 and H2.*

**Prediction 2 — P = 0.78.** The U.S. ECI, measured by the validated methodology of Document 4, shows continued decline from its 2024 level through 2028 absent a major intervention in platform incentive architecture. *Resolution (Dec 2028):* ECI(2028) < ECI(2024) under the standardized methodology. *Conditional on the ECI instrument being fielded; tests the information-layer mechanism.*

**Prediction 3 — P = 0.65.** At least three U.S. states experience significant fiscal-stress events (general-obligation credit downgrade, declared fiscal emergency, or failure to meet scheduled pension obligations) between 2026 and 2030. *Resolution (Dec 2030):* documented occurrence in ≥3 distinct states.

**Prediction 4 — P = 0.42.** A significant anti-systemic political formation distinct from the current Republican Party achieves double-digit popular-vote share in at least one statewide or federal election by 2030. *Resolution (Nov 2030):* any non-Democratic, non-Republican candidate exceeding 10% in a Senate, gubernatorial, or presidential election. *The lower probability reflects genuine uncertainty about whether the two-party structure absorbs anti-systemic demand.*

**Prediction 5 — P = 0.83.** The U.S. CSI does not return to a moderate-stress regime by 2030 absent a structural policy intervention sufficient to reverse at least two of: wealth-concentration trajectory, bottom-quintile real-wage growth, institutional trust, or the EOI. *Resolution (Dec 2030):* assessed against the computed CSI under Document 4's methodology. *The most structurally grounded prediction, resting on the slowness of these variables relative to political cycles.*

A note on reflexivity (Document 2 §8.1): publishing these predictions may influence the actors they concern. The discipline treats this as unavoidable and addresses it through transparency; notably, an outcome that falsifies Prediction 5 — a structural correction — is one the author would welcome.

## 8. Limitations and future research

**Estimated, not computed.** The principal limitation is the one stated throughout: the CSI here is illustrative. Converting it to a computed result requires Document 4's pipeline — the coverage-restricted baseline, the corrected χ²/Hotelling thresholds, the per-dimension-normalized headline, and uncertainty propagation. Until then, this paper is a structured argument with indicative magnitudes, not a measurement.

**Epistemic-layer measurement.** The ECI is the weakest block: one component is reconstructed/estimated before ~2012, the other has no instrument yet. Prediction 2 is conditional on building it — and the instrument it requires (the FCR) is now specified and pre-registered in **Document 9**, so fielding it is what makes Prediction 2 live. This is the program's highest-priority empirical investment (Document 4 §5).

**Baseline sensitivity.** All z-scores depend on the 1948–1973 baseline. Sensitivity analyses against 1948–1968 and 1950–1980 baselines are required; the original reported these as broadly shape-preserving but level-shifting by ~15–25%, indicating the trajectory is robust to the choice while specific thresholds are not.

**The convergence-versus-diffusion caveat.** Because the industrial nation-state is a weak convergence case (Document 3 §5), structural claims that lean on its being a well-attested attractor with characteristic instabilities should be appropriately hedged.

**Sample-of-one comparison.** Weimar generates hypotheses; it does not validate. Genuine cross-civilizational validation requires the computed retrodiction set of Document 6 (late Roman Republic, French Revolution, Soviet collapse, 1848), against the 80%-correct-characterization bar of the charter.

**Reflexivity.** Treated as an unavoidable feature addressed by transparency (§7).

## 9. Conclusion

This paper has applied Civilodynamics to the United States, 2000–2024, and registered five falsifiable predictions. Its central claim is the structural-primacy hypothesis: that the six stress blocks were jointly elevated and rising before the 2015–2016 developments, that anti-systemic emergence is therefore better read as a structurally probable output of a high-stress state than as a candidate-specific anomaly, and that this — together with three more contested sub-hypotheses — can be stated precisely enough to be wrong.

The paper is careful about what it has and has not shown. The CSI values are illustrative pending computation; the Weimar comparison generates hypotheses rather than validating; the contested causal claims are demoted to operationalized sub-hypotheses with stated falsifiers; and candidate-centered and contingent explanations are credited as operating within, not against, the structural account. What distinguishes the approach is not the confidence of its numbers — which are provisional — but the structure of its commitments: a measurable stress state, a falsifiable set of hypotheses, and a registered, Brier-scorable predictive record that the coming cycles will test. Whether the specific predictions prove right or wrong, that structure is what separates this from event-level commentary, and the resolution dates of §7 are when the framework's first real empirical verdict arrives.
